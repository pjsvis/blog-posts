## These Advices Changed Everything for Me

8 min read

Aug 1, 2026

Kurt Vonnegut in 1965 (URL.png))

**Kurt Vonnegut is a writer who knows how to stay with you.**

If you’ve ever read one of his novels, you know what I mean. His writing makes you smile at the absurdity of it all, but a second later, it leaves you staring into the void, wondering what the true meaning of being here really is. He mastered the art of words and was a generous teacher, which is why he used to remind his students that their first job is to grab the reader’s attention and their second is to take care of it so they don’t leave. You have to give them an addictive story.

It doesn’t matter whether you’re writing your next short story, screenplay, novel, or newsletter — you’ve probably faced the same questions that haunt me in the morning: How do I connect this idea with the person on the other side? How do I make them stay?

Today, I want to break down with you the **seven writing rules Kurt shared** in the preface to his book _Bagombo Snuff Box_, and tell you how they’ve helped me shape the rhythm of everything I write.

**Let’s take it step by step.**

## 1\. Use people’s time in a way that makes them feel it was time well spent

Time is the scarcest and most valuable resource we have. Deep down, it is our life. Reading is one way of spending that time, perhaps one that is increasingly endangered in an age of endless short-form video content. For this reason, your reader should leave knowing they received something they didn’t have when they arrived.

Don’t think of anything too lofty. It could be something as simple as:

-   A lesson
-   An emotion
-   A practical idea
-   A new perspective

This applies to both fiction and nonfiction.

Think of movies like _Interstellar_, which gives you a profound reflection on time, love, and the future of humanity; or Homer’s _Odyssey_, which connects us with Greek mythology through an epic adventure filled with emotion; or even this article of mine, which aims to give you a practical insight for your writing.

**Always write with this value proposition in mind.**

If, when you finish your work, you can’t point to the benefit of reading it, revise the work.

## 2\. Give the reader at least one character to root for

It doesn’t matter whether your protagonist is your own audience trying to get through a gray day, a warrior in a distant world, or simply an inanimate object.

We all need someone to accompany us through the fog. Only when we get the reader emotionally invested will they be willing to hold that character’s hand through every stumble along the way. This is also a key principle that the artists at Pixar emphasize when making their successful films.

And yes, this is obvious in fiction, but it remains useful in nonfiction.

When I write an article, or when you examine the successes of the genre, one key to success is weaving it together with a story. We empathize because the author strips themselves bare and tells us about their process from the same mud we’re standing in today, or because they bring forward the experience of someone who has already climbed out of that mud. It’s simple: we don’t fall in love with data; we fall in love with people and stories.

-   **On the most intimate level:** We see ourselves in the vulnerability and hope trapped within _The Diary of a Young Girl_.
-   **In personal development:** We see ourselves in the mice from _Who Moved My Cheese?_, dealing with the fear of change.
-   **In psychology and survival:** We see ourselves in Viktor Frankl in _Man’s Search for Meaning_, looking for purpose in the middle of chaos.

If there’s no “someone” to hold on to, it’s very hard for the reader to stay.

## 3\. Every character should want something, even if it’s only a glass of water

Desire is the heartbeat that keeps a story alive.

Think of _The Big Lebowski_: an entire movie revolving around a man who simply wants to get his rug back. Now, every time you review your paragraphs, ask yourself: What is this character really looking for? Answering that question gives the plot a clear direction at all times.

-   **In _Finding Nemo_:** Everything boils down to a father who simply wants to find his son.
-   **In _Gladiator_:** The driving force behind an entire empire is a man who only wants to avenge his family and return home.
-   I**n _The Lord of the Rings_**: Despite its epic scale, the plot is as simple as two friends who want to throw a ring into a volcano.

You can make things as intricate, profound, and expansive as you want, but at their core, things are quite simple. Have a clear premise and build around it.

## 4\. Every sentence should either reveal character or advance the action

This is one of the best editing principles there is. In short fiction, where Kurt was an expert, if a sentence doesn’t reveal something about a character or move the story forward… simply remove it.

Your reader will thank you.

And in nonfiction, the same rule applies: **every sentence should move toward a new idea or develop the value of what you’re explaining.** If a paragraph doesn’t deepen the subject or take you to the next step, it’s filler.

The masters of nonfiction get straight to the point and make every line count:

-   **In _The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People_:** Stephen Covey doesn’t waste time; each principle is broken down with clarity and pragmatism.
-   **In _Atomic Habits_:** James Clear cuts out the fluff to give you a manual where every line is a precise instruction for using the method.

Conciseness and clarity don’t take away depth. Quite the opposite: in many works where the reader’s goal is to want to know more, they amplify it. Read with your scissors in hand and cut everything that doesn’t take the reader toward what they want to know.

## 5\. Start as close to the end as possible

**This is crucial in short formats such as short stories and newsletter-style nonfiction.**

Cut the unnecessary preambles and let your reader fall straight into the heart of the story. Starting in the middle of the action, what narrative calls _in medias res_, is exciting for you as the writer and transfers that same pull to the person reading.

It’s the key to succeeding in short formats like the short story, and it applies to nonfiction too.

Long introductions are obstacles in the path of a modern reader who lives in a hurry. Your opening should immediately make the value of staying clear. Don’t beat around the bush — present the problem, promise the solution, and get started.

Look at the introduction to this article as an example. In just three paragraphs, I told you:

-   Kurt Vonnegut was a master at hooking readers.
-   He taught his students to do the same.
-   Are you struggling to connect people with your ideas and make them stay?
-   Stay, because here are his seven expert rules to solve that problem.

**If the topic interested you, you were hooked instantly.**

## 6\. Be a sadist

No matter how sweet and innocent your characters are, make terrible things happen to them.

This is also the golden rule of Pixar movies: We fall in love with the characters’ struggle, not their easy success. It’s only in the storms that we get to see what they’re made of, and we love them because they reflect our own imperfections — we see people who, deep down, are just like you and me.

It’s what I was saying earlier about nonfiction: connect with stories of people who climbed out of the mud and explain how they did it so they can help you.

## 7\. Write to please just one person

There’s an unfounded idea that publishing on the internet means speaking to an auditorium full of people.

**That’s not how it works.**

If you try to make the entire world fall in love with your work, your voice will become diluted. Look at the content that goes viral: it tends to be superficial because it needs to appeal to the lowest common denominator to reach everyone. In that process, it loses all its charisma because it has to be capable of touching everyone — and every person is a universe when you go deep enough.

Instead, if you write with one specific person in mind — with their doubts, their fears, what they need to read today — something magical happens. The magic of platforms like Substack is that success isn’t measured in millions of empty clicks. The artist’s value lies in connecting with a small community of souls who resonate with your art, support your work, and take part in the process. The ongoing exchange of comments and events creates a bond between you and your readers, between all of us.

**Think about your ideal reader, write only for them, and let the others find their way to you.**
