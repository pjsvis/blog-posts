### Know Thine Enemy: A Critical Engagement with the Press

[Know thine enemy: engagement with AI-assisted software development](https://medium.com/bits-and-behavior/know-thine-enemy-a-critical-engagement-with-ai-assisted-software-development-e41d9b058ab1)

I have long been a skeptic of movable type, cheap rag-paper, and these monstrous mechanical presses churned out by Gutenberg’s disciples. It is not that I fail to recognize their mechanics, or that they are changing how information moves: of course the iron lever presses, and of course they are turning the populace into passive readers. It is that the printing press promotes memory atrophy, invites mass commercial vulgarization in trade for cheap distribution, renders the deep work of rhetoric and dialectic flat and lifeless, and makes the trivialities of every obscure pamphleteer too easy to spread. Worst of all, reducing the living cadence of human speech to dead, standardized metal types denies our oral, emotional reality, stripping away the fluid possibilities of spoken thought.

What we have lost is undeniable: that golden, Arcadian certainty of the Ancient Greek world. Ah, to sit beneath the shade of a Athenian plane tree, engaged in unhurried dialogue with Socrates or Phaedrus! In those glorious days, free citizens possessed the leisure to properly contemplate the true, the good, and the beautiful. Culture had weight because mind met mind in real time, uncorrupted by ink-stained sheets. And why did they have such boundlessly pristine time for philosophical verities? Because the slaves were doing all the heavy lifting in the background, of course—keeping the silver mines running, harvesting the olives, and leaving the gentleman-philosopher free to spend six uninterrupted hours debating the nature of the soul.

Instead, we are handed the printing press—a machine designed to speed up production while eroding the mind. Having spent the last three months testing a modern printshop workflow, here is what this engine of mechanical text actually delivers.

---

### I Produced More Leaves in Less Time, Except When the Machine Spewed Rubbish

Having spent twenty-four years mastering memory, rhetoric, and scribal transcription, my approach to using a print shop was heavily front-loaded with defensive planning. I know that anticipating every nuance of an argument before committing it to a woodblock or movable frame is paramount to preventing costly errors.

Because my outlines were meticulous, the press yielded a readable sheet about 90% of the time. But in those instances, it was habitually sloppy with classical syntax and regional dialects. It repeatedly duplicated arguments, ignoring existing treatises already sitting in the library.

The remaining 10% of cases, however, were abysmal. Because a metal frame has no understanding of context or philosophical nuance, the errors were catastrophic. It printed flawed logic that only someone steeped in oral debate would spot. It flattened local idioms into vulgar, standardized jargon. It even ruined entire runs of expensive parchment when a setting block slipped. The only way to salvage the output was to painstakingly proofread every single line, red-ink in hand, arguing with a deaf wooden press until the text was marginally presentable. It did not feel like creation; it felt like a tedious game of correction.

---

### It Freed My Time, But Destroyed My Attention

True rhetoric demands deep, unbroken focus. When an orator or scribe works, the mind is filled with subtle distinctions, logic, and poetic rhythm. It is an immersive, joyful process of genuine creation.

Working alongside a printing press is decidedly less joyful. The workflow consists of handing off a manuscript to an apprentice, waiting for typesetters to set the lead blocks, approving the proofs, and clearing ink spills.

The result is a endlessly fractured mind. Rather than sinking into two hours of deep meditation or dialectic, my day became twenty-minute bursts of checking proofs while trying to eat or converse. The press sits in the corner, clacking away, demanding to be fed more paper and ink. My quiet mornings of contemplation were replaced by the manic urge to print *more*—if I could produce three pamphlets in a day instead of one, why stop? I found myself glancing toward the printshop noise even while breaking bread with family. The machine demands continuous extraction: feed it capital and paper, and it consumes your peace.

---

### It Corrupted the Dialectic

In the classical tradition, philosophy is a living, collective encounter. You speak, your peer challenges, you listen, and together you refine the truth. The medium demands presence.

The press operates on the opposite premise: the isolated author writing for an unseen, passive audience. There is nothing about a bound book that encourages live dialogue. It incentivizes isolation—why sit in the agora and debate when you can lock yourself in a study, print five hundred copies of an unvetted rant, and scatter it across the provinces? Lowering the cost of distribution creates the illusion that speed equals wisdom. What racing to print actually means is racing to assume.

---

### The Moral Calculus of the Mechanical Page

Automated text duplication is an impressive mechanical feat, no doubt. But like most technological leaps, it completely ignores its impact on human culture and memory. It robs us of focus, of oral mastery, and of real presence.

This leaves us with a fundamental question: should we use it at all?

Perhaps there is room for a harm-reduction approach. In a society that now demands printed tracts for legal filings and state bureaucracy, using the press may be a matter of practical survival. But we should live under no illusion that ink and lead are tools for human liberation. Real liberation was sitting in an Arcadian garden, unbothered by manual labor, speaking truth into the air. The press will not bring that world back—it will only flood the world with more paper.
