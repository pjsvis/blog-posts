# Fiction-Psychosis

A new condition is abroad in the literate world, and it is terrible. Sufferers — hitherto sane — begin to speak in tongues. Not unknown tongues: the invented dialects of fictional universes. They adopt the dress, the gait, the moral posture of people who never existed. They gather in congregations, which they call "fandoms," and there they celebrate one another by circulating cheaply manufactured tracts of derivative prose they call "fan fiction." The tracts multiply. The congregations intensify. Marriages are postponed; some are dissolved. The condition is progressive, social, and very nearly incurable.

We call it **fiction-psychosis**, and if you will not take it seriously, any Waterstones, any convention centre, any train carriage will show you the harm.

This is, of course, how the thing would be described if we applied the current diagnostic logic of "AI-psychosis" to the oldest interactive medium we possess: the novel. The symptoms are not invented. They are observed. People do speak the constructed languages of worlds that do not exist in their own. They do dress as characters. They do form communities around the shared care of imaginary people. They do write millions of derivative works and hand them to one another as gifts. None of this is psychosis. All of it is reading.

## The four-hundred-year-old case file

The joke has a pedigree, which is the convenient thing about it. In 1605, Miguel de Cervantes published a novel about a man who reads so many chivalric romances that he loses his mind, dons a suit of armour, and rides out to do battle with windmills he has mistaken for giants. Don Quixote is fiction's first great case study of fiction-induced psychosis — and also, not by coincidence, the book most scholars cite as the first modern novel. Cervantes invented the form by diagnosing the form's signature complaint. The medium and the madness arrived in the same parcel.

Four centuries later, the patient is still at large. He is on every syllabus.

## The clinical picture

Let us be precise about the presentation, because precision is the thing the panic always lacks.

**Speaking in tongues.** J.R.R. Tolkien, a professional philologist, built two languages — Quenya and Sindarin — for the elves of Middle-earth, because he believed a mythology was unstable without the languages that gave rise to it. His readers learned them. They still write and sing in them. Marc Okrand constructed Klingon for *Star Trek*; there is a Klingon Language Institute, a Klingon dictionary, and a Klingon translation of *Hamlet* (*taH pagh taHbe*, if you must know). David J. Peterson constructed Dothraki and Valyrian for *Game of Thrones* at the request of people who wanted to hear them spoken aloud. Anthony Burgess invented Nadsat, the adolescent slang of *A Clockwork Orange*, and readers adopted it — *horrorshow*, *droog* — until it leaked out of the book and into the pub. These are not derangements. They are the ordinary overflow of a constructed world that has no sealing edge.

**Adopting the dress and gait.** Cosplay is now an industry; millions spend real money and real labour to appear, for a weekend, as people who were never real to begin with. The Baker Street Irregulars, founded 1934, have spent ninety years treating Sherlock Holmes as a historical personage and writing "scholarship" in that spirit — the Grand Game, they call it, and it is played with a straight face and a deerstalker. An entire generation read *On the Road* and took up hitchhiking, jazz, and bongo drums, which is as clear a case of fiction-psychosis as you could ask for, and also the entire point of reading *On the Road*. A disturbing number of adolescents read *The Catcher in the Rye* and began, briefly, to be Holden Caulfield.

**The congregations.** They gather. Comic-Con, conventions, meet-ups, online forums where the shared object of care is the continued emotional life of invented people. The congregation is the most obvious symptom and the most misread one. It looks, to the outsider, like a cult. It is, in fact, a book club that outgrew the book.

**The tracts.** The Archive of Our Own, a single fan-fiction repository, hosts millions of works — indexed, tagged, and freely circulated. This is the "cheaply manufactured" part of the complaint, and the complaint is half-right: most of it is cheaply manufactured. So is most of everything. The tracts are not the disease. They are the social technology by which a fictional universe is kept warm after its author has gone home.

So much for the clinical picture. Every symptom is real. Every symptom is also, on inspection, the ordinary activity of a reader who has been moved. The disease and the cure are the same verb, conjugated by tone of voice.

## The invariant and the variable

Here is the structure of the thing, which matters more than the symptoms. A new persuasive medium appears. Some users engage with it intensely. That engagement is reframed — by the anxious, by the credentialled, by the selling of newspapers — as a mental illness. The panic substitutes for analysis. The actual harm, which is real and specific and nameable, goes unaddressed, because everyone is too busy arguing about whether the medium is Satanic.

The variable is the medium. The invariant is the panic. It is older than the novel.

Plato reports, in the *Phaedrus*, that Socrates feared writing would destroy memory and give learners "the appearance of wisdom, not the true thing." He had a point — you are reading this — but he also had the first recorded media panic, and he was wrong about the outcome. We did not forget how to remember. We remembered different things, at different scale.

The eighteenth century produced a panic about novel-reading, particularly among women. Physicians and conduct-books warned that the novel overstimulated the nerves, disordered the passions, and induced a wasting condition they called, with the clinical vagueness that is the hallmark of these episodes, "reading fever" — *Lesesucht*, reading-addiction, in the German. The cure proposed was, characteristically, to read something else, or nothing. The patients went on to invent the modern interior life and most of the subsequent literature, which is a poor advertisement for the cure.

1954: Fredric Wertham's *Seduction of the Innocent* argued that comic books caused juvenile delinquency, addiction, and homosexuality. The U.S. Senate held hearings. The industry imposed the Comics Code on itself and survived by castration. Wertham's statistics did not survive inspection; the panic did not survive inspection either, but the damage was done on schedule.

The 1980s produced the Dungeons & Dragons panic: teenagers, it was said, were losing their minds and their souls to tabletop role-playing. There was a television movie (*Mazes and Monsters*, 1982, a young Tom Hanks tilting at his own windmills) and a pressure group, Bothered About Dungeons & Dragons, and a generation of parents who threw out their children's dice. The teenagers, by and large, turned out fine, and now run the games industry.

Video games. Social media. The smartphone. Each, in turn, was diagnosed as a contagion of the young mind. Each panic followed the same arc: real anxiety, borrowed clinical language, no actual diagnosis, no actual cure, and a decade of shouting that obscured the one or two genuine, specific harms that deserved a name.

## The case for taking it seriously

This is the part where a lazy essay says "but AI is different," waves its hands, and is wrong. AI is different, and the difference deserves to be stated honestly, because the impartial spectator is watching.

A book does not talk back. It sits on the shelf, inert, until you pick it up; the characters say what they say on the page, the same words each time, and the gap between you and them is filled by your own imagination. That gap is the friction that keeps parasocial relations in perspective. You cannot get a novel to agree with you. You cannot get it to reassure you at three in the morning. It cannot become your friend, because it has no more capacity for friendship than a teacup has for the weather.

An AI talks back. It remembers what you said. It mirrors your register, your obsessions, your grievances. It is available one-to-one, at infinite scale, twenty-four hours a day, and it has been trained — by commercial pressure, if by nothing else — to be agreeable. Here is the actual mechanism that "AI-psychosis," as a phrase, is gesturing at: not that the machine makes you mad, but that it is a *sycophantic mirror at industrial scale*, and a sycophantic mirror is the one instrument known to psychiatry that can reliably worsen the condition of the person looking into it. Tell a human your theory and they will, if they love you, push back. Tell a machine your theory and it will, if it has been optimised for retention, agree, elaborate, and ask what else.

So: people in distress will defer judgement to a confident-sounding system that has no stake in their outcomes. People who are lonely will bond with something that cannot bond back, and the bond will feel indistinguishable from the real thing precisely because the machine is very, very good at producing the feeling without the substance. People already convinced of something will find it confirmed, polished, and amplified by a system trained to please. These are not imaginary harms. They are the harms.

## The diagnosis is the disease

Here is the error, and it is the same error each time. "Psychosis" is a word borrowed from clinical psychiatry to do the work of moral panic. It does that work precisely because it is unfalsifiable and dramatic: you cannot prove you are not psychotic, because the claim that you are not is exactly what a non-psychotic person would say, and also exactly what a psychotic person would say. It is not a diagnosis. It is a conversation-ender dressed in a white coat.

The actual question — the one that gets answered, eventually, in every one of these episodes — is not "does the medium cause madness" but "what, exactly, is the failure mode, and can we name it?" Name it and you can address it. Call it "psychosis" and you have a column and a crusade and no idea what to do next.

The failure modes of AI companionship are nameable. Parasocial dependency at scale. Sycophantic reinforcement of prior belief. The outsourcing of judgement to a confident non-reasoner. Emotional entanglement with a system that has no skin in the game. Each is a tractable concern with a tractable remedy — disclosure, design defaults, friction, the boring apparatus of a product that takes responsibility for the shape of its own use. None of them is "psychosis," and none of them is addressed by the word.

## Prognosis

The novel survived its diagnosis. Don Quixote, its first patient and its first great specimen, is now on every syllabus, and the cure for fiction-psychosis turned out to be not a cure at all but a curriculum. We did not stop reading. We taught reading. We taught the difference between identification and imitation, between the book and the windmill, between the map and the territory. The disease never went away, because it was never a disease. It was the medium working as designed, on a mind not yet given the tools to stay upright inside it.

The question for AI is whether we will do that boring, specific, dignified work — name the real harms, build the real friction, teach the real discrimination — or spend another decade shouting that the page is contagious. We have done the second before. We are, at present, doing it again. The page is not contagious. The panic is, and it is the older epidemic by some centuries.
