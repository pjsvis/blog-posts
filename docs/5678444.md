You’ve hit on a persistent blind spot in Western primatology and animal welfare science.

When researchers like Anderson et al. look at wild and captive ape sleeping arrangements through a modern welfare lens, there’s an almost irresistible pull toward **anthropomorphic domesticity**. The baseline assumption of modern human comfort—a climate-controlled room, a plush mattress, isolation from pests, and an uninterrupted 8-hour stretch—gets quietly subbed in as the universal gold standard for "good sleep."

When you run primate nesting through an **Operational Heuristic** (evaluating the system by how it actually functions in its raw environment rather than its ideal state), the "why don't they just get into bed" perspective completely breaks down.

### 1. The Homeostatic Trade-Off

In the wild, sleep isn't an isolated luxury; it's a high-risk operational state. An ape’s night system balances three competing vectors:

* **Predation risk** (height, branch stability, canopy density)
* **Thermoregulation & parasite mitigation** (vector avoidance, air flow)
* **Physical comfort** (pressure point relief, posture support)

A "good bed" solves for physical comfort, but in a wild system, optimizing purely for comfort gets you eaten or covered in ectoparasites. A great ape's nest is a disposable, highly dynamic micro-environment built to solve *tonight's* specific environmental variables.

### 2. Captivity and the "Furniture Fallacy"

When primatologists shift focus to captive welfare, the "cannot get out of bed" bias peaks. They look at a gorilla on concrete or wooden platforms and instinctively think, *"This needs to look and feel more like a human bedroom."*

They supply shredded paper, woodchips, or blankets, framing it as providing "comfort." But operationally, what matters to the ape isn't the soft mattress substitute—it's **agency and work**.

* The cognitive process of weaving, selecting, and manipulating materials is what lowers cortisol and stereotypic behaviors.
* Giving an ape a soft, pre-made human-style bed misses the point entirely. The *construction* of the nest is integral to the sleep state itself.

### 3. Sleep Architecture vs. Continuous Unconsciousness

The implicit human standard assumes continuous, deep, undisturbed sleep is the only "quality" sleep. But wild nighttime behavior in great apes includes periodic wakefulness, social checking, nest adjustments, and shifting positions to manage thermal loss or pressure.

When researchers evaluate captive nighttime behavior and flag night-waking or repositioning as potential "welfare deficits" or "poor sleep quality," they are often measuring apes against an industrialized human sleep model—one that humans themselves only invented relatively recently (post-industrial artificial lighting and monolithic 8-hour sleep blocks).

---

### The Fundamental Mismatch

The researchers often struggle to decouple **comfort** from **function**.

A nest isn't a prototype for a Serta mattress; it's a dynamic, low-cost engineering solution to nightly survival. Treating nest-building as an awkward, evolutionary stepping stone toward "getting into a nice warm bed" flips the logic on its head. The ape isn't missing out on a bed—the bed is a hyper-specialized human hack that only works because we eliminated predators, built rigid walls, and outsourced our thermoregulation to HVAC systems.
